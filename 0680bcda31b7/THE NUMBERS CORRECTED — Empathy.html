03 · Two things inside your workbook that disagree

You asked whether anyone had actually looked at how you arrived at your numbers. These are what looking produced. Neither is presented as an error — both may be deliberate — but nothing in the file labels them, so a reader cannot tell which reading the P&L uses.

◆ One — merch costs appear twice, at two different rates

The Assumptions tab sets merch cost of goods at 45%. The Unit Economics tab prices a merchandise order at $65 with a 71% variable cost, leaving $18.85 of contribution. Both can be true if the 45% is goods alone and the 71% adds returns, shipping and platform — but that distinction is not written anywhere in the file. It matters because merch is your largest single line by 2031 at $750,000, and the two rates produce materially different contribution.

◆ Two — the 2026 subscription line assumes a full year inside a four-month one

Your model control sets 2026 to four active months, September through December. Unit Economics prices the audio subscription at $96 per YEAR against 260 subscribers — $24,960, which is your stated 2026 subscription revenue. But a subscriber who joins in September pays four months, not twelve. At $8/month across four months, 260 subscribers produce $8,320. Reaching $25,000 in four months needs roughly 781 subscribers.

Membership does not have this problem — an annual fee is paid up front, so $120 × 750 inside a four-month window is coherent. Merch is one-off and likewise fine. It is specifically the RECURRING line, where an annualised price meets a partial year.

WHY THIS ONE IS WORTH CATCHING NOW: an investor who divides $25,000 by $96 arrives at 260, and then asks how 260 people paid a full year in four months. That question should not be heard for the first time in the room. Is 260 an exit-rate at December, or an annualised average? The two readings differ by roughly three times.
